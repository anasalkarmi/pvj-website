Phoenix criteria for paediatric sepsis and septic shock - what is new?

  • RCPCH Conference 2024
  • Birmingham
  • Published 30 December 2024
  • 2:04 min
  • Professor Mark PetersUCL Great Ormond Street Institute for Child Health

Key points

  • The Phoenix sepsis criteria use large-scale data to help tell children with sepsis from children who are ill from something else.
  • Sepsis is characterised by organ dysfunction associated with infection that carries an additional risk of death, where it used to be a very inclusive category.
  • The criteria are intended to support epidemiological studies and clinical trials, but are not a screening tool for who should be treated.

Professor Mark Peters, UCL Great Ormond Street Institute for Child Health, provides an overview of the Phoenix criteria for paediatric sepsis and septic shock. The Phoenix sepsis criteria for sepsis and septic shock in children were derived and validated by the international SCCM Paediatric Sepsis Definition Task Force using a large international database and survey, systematic review and meta-analysis, and modified Delphi consensus approach. A Phoenix Sepsis Score of at least 2 identified potentially life-threatening organ dysfunction in children younger than 18 years with infection, and its use has the potential to improve clinical care, epidemiological assessment, and research in paediatric sepsis and septic shock around the world. This interview took place at the RCPCH conference 2024 in Birmingham, UK.

So I was talking today about the Phoenix sepsis criteria, which are an attempt to use large scale data to improve our ability to discern patients with sepsis from children who are ill from something else. This is the output from about 5 years' work from a collaboration led by the Society of Critical Care Medicine in the US, and essentially what it's done is characterised a degree of organ dysfunction associated with infection that carries an additional risk of death. That's a big and complicated sentence, but it's a contrast to what sepsis was before, which was a very inclusive category that included children who wouldn't even be sick enough to be in hospital.

So we now have two papers published in [unclear] at the end of January that describe how 3 million, 3 and a half million patient encounters from around the world, of children coming into accident emergency rooms or on the ward, were classified by the presence of organ dysfunction. And those that had a degree of organ dysfunction that scores [unclear] two points on this novel score had a greatly increased risk of death. And the idea is that that will assist with epidemiological studies, it'll assist with clinical trials to make sure they've got the same population of patients included wherever they've come from.

But I should be clear what it is not is a screening tool for who should be treated for potential to deteriorate with infection. That's a much harder holy grail that we're still searching for. But I think it does do something useful to standardise doctors who are notoriously variable in what they mean when they say the word sepsis currently. So I think it is a development, yes.
