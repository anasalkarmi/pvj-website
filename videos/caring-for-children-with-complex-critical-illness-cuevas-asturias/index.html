Caring for children with complex critical illness

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 4:42 min

Key points

  • Of 22 UK centres that replied, 16 had a working model of multidisciplinary care for children with complexity, but there was no consensus on how such a child is defined.
  • Most PICUs have no specific time or funding for children with complexity, and 18 of the 22 units had no youth worker allocated.
  • Staff reported a high level of stress and burnout associated with the heavy workload of these multidisciplinary meetings and ongoing care.

Dr Sofia I. Cuevas-Asturias, Consultant in Paediatric Intensive Care, discusses multidisciplinary care for children with complex critical illness. She describes UK mixed-methods work with PICSTAR examining how these children are defined and supported, and how teams organise their care. The interview explores access to specialist resources, different models of multidisciplinary working and the challenges faced by staff. Dr Cuevas-Asturias reflects on areas of growing provision as well as remaining gaps, highlighting the need to consider the experiences of children, families and professionals when developing services.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

Hi, my name is Sofia. I'm a paediatric intensive care consultant and I was presenting a study that myself and Donna Austin are doing as part of a mixed method study on children with complex critical illness in the UK. We did it alongside the PICSTAR team, who is a UK trainee research network. And it was done by Stephanie Clark, Manisha Kumar, Donna Austin and myself.

The study was looking at working models within the UK within paediatric intensive care and looking at how the provision for children of complexity is dealt with and looked after within the UK. So we were specifically looking at the multidisciplinary team and are they identifying children with complexity? If they are identifying children with complexity, what are the multidisciplinary team provision that they're putting in place within PICUs?

Our study found that, well, we managed to get 22 centres to reply. We had three different levels of survey that were sent out to medics, nurses and allied health professionals. Out of the 22 centres, 16 of them had a working model of care for multidisciplinary team specifically for children with complexity. There wasn't a consensus on how a child with complexity within the UK in intensive care is currently defined. And that is a work that I'm currently on, as an aside, undertaking as part of my MD, and I hope to have a consensus definition by the end of the academic year.

But the working definition that each of these units had mainly consisted on a prolonged length of stay, clinician discretion, family wanting to have a team named or led by either the nurses or the allied health professionals. Once they had a multidisciplinary team named, we also asked what do you provide and are there any provision specifically for each specialty within the working roles to have time allocated for children with complexity within the UK.

The findings were that actually the vast majority of PICUs don't have time specifically or funding specifically for children with complexity, but they had lots of different allied health professionals within the UK allocated for PICU specifically but not for complexity within PICU. There was also an interesting finding that 18 out of the 22 units didn't have any youth worker allocated at all to complexity or to PICU full stop.

We also then asked what was the, did they feel that the workload was good, and it was a very open-ended question, and what were positives or negatives of having the multidisciplinary work for complexity. The findings showed that there was a high level of stress and a high level of burnout associated with the heavy workload with these multidisciplinary meetings and ongoing care.

A lot of children with complexities stay within PICU for long lengths of stay, have very complex outcomes, have family dynamics that can be beautiful but sometimes can cause conflict, and clinicians at the moment and nursing teams and allied health professionals often don't get allocated extra [unclear] or time to have that time to be really in depth and spend it with the families. And so it was unsurprising to find that staff stress and burnout was a high factor.

So in conclusion, it was really nice to see that there is a growing 16 out of our 22 units have multidisciplinary teams available for children with complexity, and there is a growing awareness of complexity within PICU in the UK, but there is still yet lots to be done. Thank you very much.
