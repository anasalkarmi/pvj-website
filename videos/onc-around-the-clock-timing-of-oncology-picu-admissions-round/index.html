Onc Around the Clock: timing of oncology PICU admissions

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 3:03 min
  • Professor Jonathan RoundProfessor of Practice in Clinical Education and Consultant Paediatric Intensivist

Key points

  • PICANet data on unplanned oncology admissions to English PICUs from 2008 to 2022 showed a bulge in the afternoon and evening and far fewer admissions at night.
  • Mortality was around 15% for overnight admissions against around 10% for afternoon admissions, and higher than expected for similar levels of dependency.
  • Possible explanations include patient selection, delayed decision making and under-triage, and these are speculation that prospective research would need to test.

Professor Jonathan Round, Professor of Practice in Clinical Education and Consultant Paediatric Intensivist, provides insights into research examining the timing of unplanned oncology admissions to paediatric intensive care. Drawing on PICANet data, he describes differences in admission patterns and mortality across the day and night. He explores possible explanations, including variation in admission decisions and patient selection, while distinguishing these hypotheses from conclusions the available data can establish. The interview highlights the value of large datasets in identifying questions for further investigation and the need for prospective research to understand the pathways and outcomes of children with cancer who require intensive care.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

Great. It's with great honour that I'm presenting my work this afternoon, and my work in my research group called METRO. And it's all around the admission data for oncology patients that are coming in unplanned to PICUs in England. I'm very grateful to the guys at PICANet for giving me this data. So I've got 14 years of unplanned oncology admissions from 2008 to 2022. And what we were trying to do is try and see if there was any variation over a 24-hour period depending on what time people came into the unit.

So we were first of all very surprised, because we thought that probably people would be roughly coming in fairly uniformly throughout a 24-hour period, but we were quite surprised to find there was a big bulge in the afternoon and in the evening and far, far less people came in at night. So it's about half the numbers per hour of this particular group of patients. Interesting to start off with.

Then when you look at the mortality, you find that the people coming in overnight have a mortality of around 15% as against around 10% of those coming in in the afternoon. So again, these are quite big differences, statistically important differences too. When you compare those against what normally happens to similar sort of people with similar levels of dependency measured on the [unclear] score, you find actually it's two or three times higher than you'd expect for similar groups.

Now that's kind of as far as we got to, because actually then you begin to get into very detailed data that we actually don't have in the PICANet database. But it's a very interesting finding because probably there's a lot of excess mortality that you could potentially do something about in that group, and that takes us on to further research that we're planning to undertake in a prospective manner.

But it's always nice to speculate. And the two bits of speculation are that potentially there might be lots of people being admitted in the afternoon who don't really need to be admitted, in other words diluting the patients who are going to die. So it looks as though their mortality is a bit better. What we can also see is that there's a real bulge of people coming in from 10 till 2 p.m., and it would suggest that these sorts of people are actually probably delayed from coming in earlier in the morning. In other words, delayed decision making.

And the last bit of speculation is around those who need to be intubated, because lots more people need to be intubated in the afternoon than in overnight. So actually they're deteriorating after they're coming in. It suggests they've been rather undertriaged. So, it's kind of work in progress, but I think you'd find the variation a very interesting finding. Thank you.
