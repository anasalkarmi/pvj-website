Rethinking gastric residual monitoring in PICU

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 1:29 min
  • Professor Lyvonne TumeProfessor of Critical Care Nursing

Key points

  • A randomised controlled trial in 24 UK and one Swiss PICU found that not measuring gastric residual was no worse than measuring it for time on ventilation or mortality.
  • Not measuring gastric residual was also beneficial for energy target achievement at 72 hours, though the impact was minimal.
  • Many long-standing intensive care practices are probably ineffective and low value, and should be challenged to avoid wasting resources and staff time or causing harm.

Professor Lyvonne Tume, Professor of Critical Care Nursing, provides insights into the GASTRIC-PICU trial and the value of questioning established routines in paediatric intensive care. She discusses the study of gastric residual monitoring during enteral feeding and the outcomes that matter to children, families and staff. The interview considers the potential consequences of continuing practices without clear evidence of benefit, including their effect on nutrition, staff time and patient care. Professor Tume reflects on how research can help teams reconsider familiar procedures and identify care that may no longer be necessary.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

So I'm going to talk a bit today about the GASTRIC-PICU trial, which was a trial led by myself as a intensive care nurse. This was a practice that we had historically done. So I wanted to challenge this. There had been evidence in adults that it wasn't really useful.

So we did a randomised control trial, recruited 4,700 children from across 24 UK and one Swiss PICU over the last two and a half years, and we found that not measuring gastric residual was no worse nor inferior than measuring gastric residual in terms of length of time on ventilation and any impact on mortality. On the upside, we also found that it was actually beneficial in terms of energy target achievement at 72 hours and it was minimal impact, but it was nevertheless a positive impact.

And I think I guess the key message from this trial is that there's lots of things that we've done in intensive care for many years that are probably ineffective, low value, but we've done them historically and we've never really challenged those practices. And I think this is the future of critical care. How much more we can make in terms of discovery, it's unclear. But what we can do is try and look at not wasting resources, not wasting staff time, and not actually having an impact of harm, potentially harm on children by doing things that we don't need to do.
