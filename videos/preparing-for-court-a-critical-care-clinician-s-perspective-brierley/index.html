Preparing for court: a critical-care clinician’s perspective

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 4:16 min
  • Dr Joe BrierleyConsultant Paediatric Intensivist and Director of Paediatric Bioethics at Great Ormond Street Hospital

Key points

  • Clinicians going to court are witnesses to fact, and the key advice is generally not to panic because it is not as scary as people think.
  • A witness statement is evidence and should be clear, written in language anyone can understand, with medical jargon explained.
  • When giving evidence, listen to the question, answer it to the best of your ability without going further, and stay respectful to the family.

Dr Joe Brierley, Consultant Paediatric Intensivist and Director of Paediatric Bioethics at Great Ormond Street Hospital, provides insights into preparing to give evidence in court. He discusses the role of witness statements, presenting factual information clearly and understanding the questions clinicians may face. The interview considers practical preparation, respectful communication and maintaining constructive relationships with families during difficult proceedings. Dr Brierley reflects on the particular challenges of cases involving life-sustaining treatment and the importance of explaining clinical care in language that the court can understand.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

So hi everybody, Joe Brierley. I am going to let you know a little bit about preparing to go to court, my talk here at PCCS and the Lord's Cricket Centre. So the first thing for me to say is generally don't panic. It is not as scary as people think it is.

Generally the key thing to remember is you are witnesses to fact. So most of us go to court in a situation where the court's been asked to make a decision about a child's best interests, often for intensivists about withdrawal of life sustaining treatment, which is really hard because for the family they're associating that with a child dying from the act you do of stopping the ventilator, and actually in reality we feel that's much more about the child dying from the underlying condition. The court would say, and judges when they speak about this say, it's the hardest decision they ever have to make. We should reflect on that and acknowledge it and realise that for the family and the judge, this is a very big deal. It is for us too.

The key thing for us is to be professional. Your report is your own evidence, but we work as teams. So whilst you're thinking about what the neurologist said, what the rest of your ICU team say, as a nurse, you talk about your daily experience of caring for that child and you can say, "My colleagues have written this stuff as well." As a doctor, you're often being asked what it's like making decisions for that child and why you think it's not in the child's best interest to carry on life sustaining treatment. Again, be clear. Write things down in language that anyone can understand. Explain medical jargon. The report is evidence. It is a witness statement for the court.

And your legal team will help you write it in the right way. They can't tell you what to write. This is your job. Talk to colleagues who've done it before. There are training courses to help you familiarise yourself with writing witness statements. Some of them run by our hospitals and at Great Ormond Street. Our legal team run just such a course to help you write statements.

Well, okay. Giving evidence is slightly different. Sometimes when you write a statement that's enough. The court takes that as the evidence and doesn't need you to appear. But for consultants involved in life sustaining treatment cases, indeed for nurses increasingly, the courts will want to hear your evidence first-hand. First thing, do make sure you turn up on time. There's often like an airport screening security arrangement. So don't turn up with a minute to go. Get in early. Have a nice breakfast. Talk to your barrister who's representing the trust. What time should I get here? Do you want to meet me beforehand? Again, the same as I said with statements, they can't coach you, but they can definitely help you about evidence and familiarising yourself with the courts, and courses are available to do that.

When you go into the witness box, the family are often there. Do acknowledge them. Say hello. Your evidence is for the court. So, the judge will address you. You address the judge. Although slightly strangely, the questions come from the barristers. Both barristers might be representing the family. They might be representing the child and the guardian [unclear], or they might well be representing the trust. All of those three people can ask you questions.

The key step for me is to listen to the question. Think about it. Answer the question to the best of your ability, but don't go any further. There's no need to go on and talk about other things. You don't want to waste the court's time. The court deals with matters of fact, your thoughts about the child. And there's someone who's appearing about a child they're caring for. Matters of fact and your experience of caring for that child. Sometimes they may ask you to go on and what will happen about certain ways the court will determine things. Again, feel free to talk about expert end of life care. This is what we do in the intensive care units. We are really good at this stuff.

When a court case is heard now, very often we go with the family. Relationships don't need to break down, but sometimes the family can't get to the same place as the clinical team. That's okay. Be respectful. Work with the families even on the days you go to court.
