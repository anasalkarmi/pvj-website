Swallow Vision: paediatric laryngeal ultrasound

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 1:31 min
  • Dr Mari ViviersClinical Lead for Paediatric Speech and Language Therapy

Key points

  • Speech and language therapists are developing ultrasound at the bedside to assess vocal fold mobility and sucking and swallowing function in infants and children.
  • A feasibility study found it is feasible to assess vocal fold mobility movement patterns in young children and infants on video recording via ultrasound.
  • Next steps are a larger pilot study in cohorts with vocal fold mobility impairment and two pilots on neonatal and infant sucking and swallowing protocols.

Dr Mari Viviers, Clinical Lead for Paediatric Speech and Language Therapy, provides insights into the Swallow Vision research team's work on paediatric laryngeal ultrasound. She explains how speech and language therapists are exploring ultrasound as a bedside assessment tool for infants and children, including assessment of vocal fold mobility. The interview discusses the team's feasibility work and the potential to bring assessment closer to the point of care. Dr Viviers also outlines plans for further clinical studies and the development of approaches to assessing sucking and swallowing in neonates and infants.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

I'm here at the PCCS conference today where I'm sharing our Swallow Vision research team's work around paediatric laryngeal ultrasound use. We are a team of speech therapists that are leading ultrasound use at the bedside in PICU or in other clinical groups, looking at vocal fold mobility assessment, sucking and swallowing function in infants and children.

We were particularly interested in developing ultrasound as a tool to bring these skill sets that we have to the bedside or to the point of care for assessment. Our feasibility study was just published in the Frontiers of Neurology journal and we presented those findings here today, where we found that it is feasible to assess young children and infants in vocal fold mobility movement patterns on video recording via ultrasound.

And we also found that there is quite good [unclear] reliability for laryngeal measurements that we are interested in producing for our work. So this work is hopefully in the next steps going to lead to a larger pilot study where we are going to specifically look at clinical cohorts with vocal fold mobility impairment, and then also pivoting off into two pilot studies where we are developing new neonatal and infant sucking and swallowing protocols.
