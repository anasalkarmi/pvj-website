Neonatal Oral Antibiotics at Home (NOAH) project: improving early discharge and family outcomes

Key points

  • The NOAH project switches eligible babies with suspected early-onset infection from intravenous to oral antibiotics to get them home earlier.
  • The pilot reduced bed days and improved parental satisfaction, bonding and breastfeeding, with no adverse events or reinfection.
  • A regional rollout and evaluation is planned, and all project resources can be downloaded from the Health Innovation Network South West website.

Dr David Bartle and Dr Harriet Aughey, Royal Devon University Hospital, present the Neonatal Oral Antibiotics at Home (NOAH) project. Inspired by the results of the RAIN study, which demonstrated the benefits of switching from intravenous to oral antibiotics in newborns and enabling earlier discharge, the NOAH project was developed to support babies with suspected early-onset infection who are eligible to switch to oral antibiotics. The project involved extensive collaboration with local infectious disease teams, maternity and neonatal safety initiatives, and partnerships such as the Maternity and Neonatal Safety Improvement Programme. Supported by the Health Innovation Network South West and PenARC, the pilot has yielded positive outcomes, including reduced hospital bed days, improved parental satisfaction, enhanced bonding, and better breastfeeding rates. Importantly, there have been no adverse events or cases of reinfection. The team is now planning a regional rollout and evaluation, with all project resources available for download to support implementation in other trusts. This interview took place at the RCPCH conference 2025 in Glasgow, Scotland.

So we've been working on a project which is a Neonatal Oral Antibiotics at Home project, or the NOAH project. We were really interested to hear the results of the RAIN study, which is about getting babies home earlier, switching from intravenous antibiotics to oral antibiotics and potentially getting them home 2 or 3 days earlier, which may help them with breastfeeding and obviously saves bed days.

So in order to do that, we spent a lot of time collaborating with local infectious disease people, working with the maternity and neonatal safety initiative, the [unclear] partnerships, and lots of other groups to work with. So what we've done is launched a pilot looking at babies with suspected early onset infection who would be eligible to switch to oral antibiotics, and carried that out in our hospital. And we've collaborated with our local Health Innovation Network and PenARC, who have supported that.

It's been really positive, lots of positive feedback from parents and staff. Positive results in terms of reduced bed days, parental satisfaction, improved bonding, improved breastfeeding, and also just getting parents home sooner has been really positive. We haven't had any adverse events or reinfection, which has been brilliant.

And we're now looking at a regional rollout and evaluation, again supported by our colleagues and partners, which we hope will then give trust around the country the confidence to implement this project and deliver better care at home for babies.
