Improving timely treatment of childhood status epilepticus

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 5:44 min
  • Dr Natasha LeveyConsultant General Paediatrician

Key points

  • An audit of 48 children in a North London trust found status epilepticus treatments mostly correct but the timings very poor.
  • Only 20% received their first benzodiazepine within five minutes, and no intubated child received RSI within the APLS time frame.
  • The team plans to include more hospitals and a QI bundle with electronic patient record prompts, targeted airway teaching and simulation training.

Dr Natasha Levey, Consultant General Paediatrician, provides insights into an audit of the acute management of status epilepticus in children. She discusses the challenges of documenting seizure and treatment timings, and the delays identified when local practice was compared with established guidance. The interview explores plans to broaden the study across additional services and develop a quality-improvement programme. Dr Levey outlines potential interventions, including electronic-record prompts, more reliable time recording, targeted teaching and simulation. She reflects on how these measures could support teams in recognising the time-critical nature of care and identifying where processes need to improve.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

So I'm here to tell you about the study that I've done, which is on status epilepticus. This was done in conjunction with a member of the [unclear] team, Dr. Knight. We were really interested in looking at the management of status epilepticus in the acute setting. This is because it's the most common neurological emergency in children, but we don't have that much data on how well it's managed compared to what our guidelines are.

So we know it's really important to be managed well. We have a huge amount of evidence that shows that if a seizure goes on for longer than 5 minutes, it's much more difficult to terminate. And once a seizure goes on for 30 minutes, permanent neuronal damage can take place. This is backed by the APLS guidance and how it should be managed, which is all very time-based and time critical. The APLS guidance is very well known and it's supported by the NICE guidance as well. It's not a controversial guidance at all. It's well accepted, established practice across the UK. However, despite this, we actually don't know how well we follow this guidance. So this study was to start looking at that.

So the first thing we did is we did a retrospective audit of children who came into a busy North London trust. We included children younger than 16 of age who presented with either multiple seizures or continuous seizures but fell into the bracket of status epilepticus. And then, using electronic patient records, we were able to get information from their record as to the timing of the seizures and what treatment they received and at what time and how well this fitted in with APLS management.

Now, there were two big things that came out of this. One, we found that firstly this information was very difficult to get, which is why the initial study has only included 48 children. It was very difficult to get because actually we don't document times very well at all despite having electronic patient records. The other thing is that the 48 children that we did include, their timings were documented and we cross-referenced them to make sure that they were accurate.

Now the children that we did include, we looked at both the treatments they received, and the APLS guidance tells us to use benzodiazepine and then we move on to the second line antiepileptics and then to an RSI. The treatments we used were, for the most part, correct and in line with our current guidance. However, the timings were very, very poor. And it seemed to be that this started in the prehosp setting, so treatment was delayed from the outset. So only 20% of the children we looked at received their first benzodiazepine within the expected five minute time. And then from that point on, every step was delayed, to the point that the children in the study who were intubated and ventilated, none of them received the RSI within the recommended APLS time frame.

However, even when we looked at children whose seizures started off in hospital, the management was also poor. They also received their first, second and third line treatment, for the majority of them, outside the recommended time frames.

So this has led us to want to look at two things. Firstly, we've only included 48 children in this study. We need to get more numbers. So we'd like to include more hospitals and more trusts in the study, and we're hoping by having my colleague being based at [unclear] that will be easier to include people from across the North London network.

And then the other thing that we'd like to do following this is we'd like to do a QI bundle targeting interventions to try and improve the timings. The first step that we'd like to do is introduce a prompt onto EPR. This would help us firstly to create accurate timings by timestamping when individuals get treatment. So it helps us collect data, but also it'll put timings at the forefront of people's minds. And in it, we want to introduce prompts to encourage people to start thinking about the next steps that are going to have to occur, because they're all time critical.

We did a bit of a deeper dive into some of the qualitative data as well which we collected, and we found that there was a lot of anxiety, particularly in the prehosp setting, about managing airways. So we'd like to do some targeted teaching around that. We'd also like to do some targeted sim training in the resource setting, which we're setting up now in my trust, to try and encourage the importance of timekeeping when it comes to status epilept. So that's a summary of my project, and yes, I'm hoping it's going to provide a stepping stone for a QI that will hopefully have a big impact on status epilepticus.
