Caring for teenagers after major trauma

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 2:00 min
  • Professor Akash DeepProfessor of Paediatric Critical Care and Director of PICU at King's College Hospital

Key points

  • The debate on where teenagers go after trauma is about more than the injury, resuscitation and operative treatment.
  • Care should cover psychosocial needs, safeguarding, work with the family, and getting back into school and sport.
  • An integrated approach should bring expertise to the teenager rather than moving the teenager to adult services alone.

Professor Akash Deep, Professor of Paediatric Critical Care and Director of PICU at King's College Hospital, provides insights into the debate about where teenagers should receive care after major trauma. He argues that the discussion must extend beyond the immediate injury, resuscitation and surgery to consider the young person's broader needs. The interview explores safeguarding, psychosocial support, family involvement and recovery back into everyday life. Professor Deep advocates an integrated approach that brings the appropriate expertise to the patient, recognising that adolescence is a period of continuing development and that care should support the whole journey of recovery.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

So I had an interesting debate today with one of my colleagues from Imperial College London where we talked about where should young children, where should young adults, say 14, 16, 17, where should they go after trauma. Now, of course, the debate is whether they should be nursed in adult critical care, in adult services, or in paediatric critical care and paediatric services. And we have a strong argument from the adult side to say they see more numbers. They're more equipped about dealing with this kind of age group.

But my argument has been we're not talking about an injury here, because injury takes place. You get resuscitated. You get the operative treatment. But the question is where do these children go after surgery? Because it's not only about the operative measures. It's about who takes care of their vulnerabilities. We've got a 16, 17 year old who've got a stab injury. They've got safeguarding concerns. They've got families to take care of.

And very importantly, I argue that a 16 year old just doesn't turn on the adult physiology on the 16th birthday. They grow. They are still a child at heart and we need to cater not only to their health needs, we need to cater for their psychosocial needs. We need to make sure they integrate well into their school. They go back to sports.

So it's the whole journey of a patient, of a teenager, which starts yes with a trauma call, yes with the surgeon, but it should have a holistic care, a holistic approach from the medical side, the psychosocial side, the safeguarding side, and work with the family as a team. So I think personally that there should be an integrated approach. You bring the expertise to the patient, to the teenager, rather than take the teenager to the adult service and leave him all alone.
