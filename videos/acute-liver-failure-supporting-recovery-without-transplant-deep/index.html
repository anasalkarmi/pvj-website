Acute liver failure: supporting recovery without transplant

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 1:36 min
  • Professor Akash DeepProfessor of Paediatric Critical Care and Director of PICU at King's College Hospital

Key points

  • Liver transplantation carries donor shortage and immunosuppression side effects, so the aim now is for children with acute liver failure to recover without it.
  • Three main things help: finding the aetiology and starting specific treatment, neuromonitoring and neuroprotection, and early extracorporeal therapy.
  • The extracorporeal therapy discussed includes total plasma exchange, started early and given at high volume with care that circuits do not clot.

Professor Akash Deep, Professor of Paediatric Critical Care and Director of PICU at King's College Hospital, provides insights into supporting children with acute liver failure. He reflects on the development of liver transplantation and discusses approaches that may help some children recover without needing a transplant, depending on the underlying cause. The interview covers identifying and treating the cause of liver failure, neurological monitoring and protection, and the role of extracorporeal therapies. Professor Deep highlights the importance of timely, coordinated critical care in supporting recovery while recognising that the clinical approach depends on the individual child's condition.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

I come from King's College Hospital, which is the largest liver failure centre in Europe. We do between 45 to 60 paediatric liver transplants a year and 10% of these patients get a transplant because they've got acute liver failure. And if you look at data, say, from the pre-transplantation era to now, pre-transplant 72% of children used to die because there were no liver transplants. But then liver transplant became available. We started to give liver transplant and then people started to realise the donor shortage, the use of immunosuppression. So loads of side effects related to liver transplant.

So what's happening in today's world is we are trying to make these children recover without the need for liver transplantation. Of course it depends upon underlying aetiology. But three main things which help these patients recover without liver transplant are: find an aetiology and start aetiology specific treatment, you support them with neuromonitoring and neuroprotection, but the key factor is to start extracorporeal therapy, whether it's [unclear] or total plasma exchange, early, give it high volume and make sure the circuits don't clot.

So I think it's a very, very important forward step which we take to say not everything is about acute liver failure and transplantation. A step should be taken for a child to survive without the need for liver transplantation.
