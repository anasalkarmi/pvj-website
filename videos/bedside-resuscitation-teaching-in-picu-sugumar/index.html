Bedside resuscitation teaching in PICU

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 2:24 min
  • Dr Janapriya SugumarPaediatric Registrar and PICSTAR National Trainee Education Lead

Key points

  • Snackademics delivered short resuscitation teaching sessions from a tea trolley with snacks, taken round a busy paediatric intensive care unit.
  • It began with basic life support, where a key performance indicator was lower than expected, then ran further cycles on intraosseous access and tracheostomy management.
  • Across 77 sessions engagement was high, and confidence and knowledge in basic resuscitation skills increased.

Dr Janapriya Sugumar, Paediatric Registrar and PICSTAR National Trainee Education Lead, provides insights into an educational quality-improvement project designed to refresh resuscitation skills in a busy paediatric intensive care unit. Using short teaching sessions delivered from a tea trolley, the team brought approachable, practical learning to staff at the bedside. The interview covers work on basic life support, intraosseous access and tracheostomy management, alongside the importance of frequent opportunities to practise. Dr Sugumar discusses staff engagement, confidence and knowledge, and reflects on how brief, accessible sessions can help sustain multidisciplinary education when clinical services are under pressure.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

I'm Janapriya Sugumar and I'm here to talk about our educational quality improvement project called Snackademics. The reason why we did Snackademics was to improve the resuscitation skills in our unit. The [unclear] and the American Heart Association both suggest that frequent repetitive learning is important to keep the resuscitation skills from decaying. And when we started we found that one of our key performance indicators, such as basic life support, was actually quite lower in our unit. So which is why we decided to start off with the basic life support skills as the first project.

So what we did was to make our project quite friendly and approachable. We started to do it based on a tea trolley where we had snacks on the bottom and the skills on the top. So we went around with the tea trolley around the busy paediatric intensive care unit and helped our nurses, trainees and consultants as well to refresh their skills on it. This was quite an astounding success. So we went on to run for two more cycles where we did it on intraosseous access and also we did it on tracheostomy management as well.

Doing the skills over 77 sessions taught us that it is really important for us to do it, especially when there is quite a lot of junior multidisciplinary staff who rotate quite often in our unit, and it's a very busy unit as well, and anyone who works in intensive care unit knows that it can be quite difficult to keep the educational program sustained and going, especially during winter where the pressures are quite high. So when we started doing this program during winter, targeting these sessions as tiny bedside sessions which they could get engaged quite well with and happen in a stress-free environment, we found the engagement to be quite high.

In our study we found that 99% of the people who participated in our program wanted to come back to the unit, and 100% of them said they would definitely do it again as well. So over 77 sessions we found that our confidence level increased in the basic resuscitation skills, and we found that the knowledge level also increased based on the measures we tested as well. So I would suggest that international recommendations and from our own personal experience that this project should be adopted by all the other units as well, especially when you're suffering through conducting education sessions in the winter. And I'm happy to talk about this more as well, and look forward to a publication which will soon come out in the [unclear]. Thank you.
