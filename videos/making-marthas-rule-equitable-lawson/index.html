Making Martha's Rule equitable

  • PCCS Conference 2026
  • Lord's, London
  • Published 7 November 2026
  • 2:02 min
  • Dr George LawsonPaediatrician

Key points

  • Access to independent paediatric review under Martha's rule is not equitable, especially for children in district general hospitals.
  • The pilot used the regional retrieval service to host a telephone line for escalations when a local Martha's rule escalation had not resolved concerns.
  • The pilot improved patient safety, resolved communication breakdown and raised staff confidence, and everyone giving feedback wanted it to continue.

Dr George Lawson, Paediatrician, provides insights into the North Thames Paediatric Network's work to make access to Martha's Rule more equitable. He describes a regional approach to escalation when local concerns remain unresolved, with particular relevance to children in district general hospitals. The interview explores the use of a regional telephone route, staff education and feedback to support independent review, improve communication and address patient-safety concerns. Dr Lawson reflects on the pilot's findings and the recommendations developed from this work, including opportunities to extend the model and strengthen communication training for staff managing difficult situations.

This interview took place at the PCCS Annual Scientific Meeting 2026 in London.

My name is George Lawson. I'm a paediatric registrar in infectious disease and I'm presenting the North Thames Paediatric Network's work on making Martha's rule equitable. So the reason for our pilot is that we know that Martha's rule is an important patient safety initiative, but access to independent paediatric review is not equitable and many childrens, especially in district general hospitals, don't have access to independent urgent review.

In order to address this, the North Thames Paediatric Network's solution was to deliver a combined system that used the regional retrieval service to host a telephone line that allowed regional escalations to come through. These could be triggered by district general hospitals when a local Martha's rule escalation hadn't resolved the concerns either of the staff or of the clinical team.

What we found from this, together with the escalation line, our education programme and the combined surveys that we did, was that we were able to make clinical interventions [unclear] where we improved patient safety, where we gave treatments that otherwise wouldn't be able to be given, and we also resolved communication breakdown. This had significant benefit for both the families and for staff and we saw that both on quantitative and from [unclear] qualitative feedback, where everyone that we received feedback from wanted the service to continue.

So from the service we managed to deliver benefit in patient safety. We also reduced communication issues and through our education programme we increased the awareness of staff and their confidence in using Martha's rule. We've submitted our recommendations to NHS England about how they might use this in the future.

Predominantly, we would think that this is a feasible model that can be scaled and used in other regions. And we'd also encourage them to look at the resources we've developed for education programmes and how they can use advanced communication training for staff that deal with these difficult conflict scenarios day in day out, and how they can manage those for better patient safety.
